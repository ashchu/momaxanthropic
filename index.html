Create Connections: what I keep coming back to

MoMA x Anthropic sprint. Nothing here is decided. These are the directions I keep circling and the question under each one.

Where I'm starting from

I think of connection two ways at once. As an engineer, a connection is two way. Something passes between two nodes and ideally both sides change. Socially, I think of how performative and fleeting it is in a city like NYC, and also how passive it is, the flaneur thing, where life just goes by and you take it in without ever touching it.

Under all of it, connection feels like hope to me. A way to make my mind and world bigger in the finite time I have.

1. Is a connection two way?

A connection only means something to me if something can come back. Someone leaves something, someone else finds it and answers, and now the first thing means something different. That's a loop, not just contact.

But I'm not sure it has to be two way. You can feel connected to someone through their writing, or to someone who has passed away, or to a character in a story, and none of them can answer.

Questiondoes a connection need to be reciprocal to be real?

2. How long does a connection have to last?

In NYC we pass thousands of people. Some of it is performative, the small talk, the polite nod. Some of it is just watching, looking into a window at night, hearing half a conversation on the train. I don't think that makes it meaningless. A few seconds can still stick with you for years, and some friendships fade without either person noticing.

I'm interested in the temporalness of social connections. Things that are only there for a moment, and what they leave behind. This also ties back to the memorial pond, where two strangers exchange advice and never meet.

Questionwhat makes a connection matter, how long it lasts or what it does to you?

3. Can you connect with something that isn't alive?

The line between animate and inanimate keeps getting thinner, and we lean on it more every year. Chatbot therapists, Siri, Alexa, humanoid robots. But I want to go simpler than that. A Tamagotchi. It's barely anything, and people still check on it, feed it, and feel responsible for it.

So where does the connection actually live? In the thing, or in the person? I'm not trying to argue that AI is the same as a person. I'm more curious about the gap between feeling like something is on the other side and it actually being there.

Questiondo we connect with things for what they are, or for what we put onto them?

4. Connection as a way to grow

When you connect, you get a glimpse of a new understanding. Childhood stories are the best example. They have a core that's rooted in something everyone can relate to, so they connect people who share nothing else.

The flip side is that it gets harder to grow out of what we know. As a kid I had breadth. As an adult it's depth, and the more I invest in what I know, the smaller my world gets. Connecting is one way out of that.

Questionhow do we stay open to worlds beyond our own as ours gets smaller?

5. People, place, and the village

This was my first instinct. Epistemology, we know what we know because of where we're from. Memories, past and present.

New York is one of the busiest places in the world and we're strangers to each other. I grew up loving Disneyland because it brought out something I didn't know was in me. But take the people out and it's just buildings. A place is the people in it. So what would a village look like in a city like this?

What I mean by a village: it isn't a bunch of houses close together. A village is the relationships between the people in it. Everyone knows a little about everyone. There are shared stories, rituals, and routines, like the same people at the well or the same festival every year. You belong because people know you and you know them, not because you live nearby.

A city like New York has the opposite. Millions of people, constant closeness, and almost no knowing. We're near each other and still strangers. So to be a village isn't about size or buildings, it's about people being known to each other and holding some history together.

Getting that in a city would mean making the invisible connections visible. The person on your train, the person across the hall, all of us carrying memories of home and childhood and places we miss. A village could be a place built out of those, something that doesn't exist until people put pieces of themselves in it, and where you can run into someone else's piece and find it familiar or completely new.

I don't think it has to look like a literal village. It could be abstract. The point is the feeling of belonging to something because of the people in it.

Questioncan strangers share a place without sharing the same experiences?

How this fits with what I've already made

  • Are We There Yet? turned my own travel memories into something other people could taste and look at.
  • The Dining Table put four people's very personal spaces into one shared VR room.
  • The memorial pond let strangers swap advice for 24 hours.
  • The 4D color space work is about what exists that we can't normally see.

The common move is that I take something personal and make a way for someone else to step into it.

Food for thought

  • Don't smooth out the contradictions yet. We want connection but stay passive. We live close and feel far. We attach to things that can't answer back.
  • Claude is a tool for making it, not the subject. The subject stays human.
  • It has to be digital, and doable in a week.
  • Make something that helps someone see something they wouldn't have noticed, not just an interaction.
