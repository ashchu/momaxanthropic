Create Connections: what I keep coming back to

{ home, now, prev }

MoMA x Anthropic sprint.

Connection is a way of knowing. It creates a sense of knowing in a space of unknown, whether that’s a stranger, a place, my own past or future, or something that can’t know me back. It’s how my world gets bigger in the finite time I have.

Where I’m starting from

I think of connection two ways at once. As an engineer, a connection is a channel between two nodes. Something passes through it, a signal, data, a request and a response, and ideally both sides change. I spend a lot of time building systems where things talk to each other, and I’ve started noticing how much of that language fits people too. Handshakes, feedback loops, latency, things getting lost in transmission.

Socially, I think of how performative and fleeting connection is in a city like NYC, and how passive it is too, the flaneur thing, where life goes by and you take it in without ever touching it.

I fell in love with New York because of letters to strangers. I loved that you could get a sense of knowing someone in a place where you don’t know anyone. And under all of it, connection feels like hope.

1. Does a connection need to be two way to be real?

In engineering, a two way connection has a handshake. Both sides confirm the other is there before anything really gets sent. A connection means the most to me when it works like that. Someone leaves something, someone else finds it and answers, and now the first thing means something different. That’s a feedback loop, not just contact.

But a lot of connection is closer to a broadcast. A letter to a stranger goes out with no idea if it lands, and it still matters. You can feel connected to someone through their writing, or to someone who has passed away, or to a character in a story, and none of them can answer. The memorial pond was kind of like this, two strangers exchanging advice who never meet.

2. What makes a connection matter, how long it lasts or what it does to you?

In NYC we pass thousands of people. Some of it is performative, like the small talk and the polite nod. Some of it is just watching, looking into a window at night or hearing half a conversation on the train. I don’t think that makes it meaningless. A few seconds can stick with you for years, and some friendships fade without either person noticing.

In systems, a signal can be really short and still change the state of everything downstream. I think people work like that. I’m interested in the temporalness of social connections, things that are only there for a moment, and what they leave behind.

3. Can I connect with myself across time?

Past me, present me, future me. Writing to your future self is kind of like an async message. You send it now and it gets read later by someone who’s changed. Memory works the other way, where you’re pulling from an old version of yourself and trying to make sense of it with who you are now.

We know what we know because of where we come from. So connecting back to an earlier version of myself, or forward to who I want to be, feels like a way of figuring out what’s actually true about me. I wonder if connection is one of the ways we get at truth in general. Things feel more real when they pass between two points, even if both points are me.

4. Do we connect with things for what they are, or for what we put onto them?

The line between animate and inanimate keeps getting thinner and we lean on it more every year. Chatbot therapists, Siri, Alexa, humanoid robots. But I want to go simpler than that. A Tamagotchi. As an engineer, I know it’s basically a handful of states and timers. It’s barely anything, and people still check on it, feed it, and feel responsible for it. I felt that too.

So where does the connection live, in the thing or in the person? I’m not trying to say AI is the same as a person. I’m more curious about the gap between feeling like something is on the other side and it actually being there. Knowing how something works doesn’t seem to stop us from feeling something toward it.

5. Can strangers share a place without sharing the same experiences?

This was my first instinct. I grew up loving Disneyland because it brought out something I didn’t know was in me. But take the people out and it’s just buildings. A place is the people in it.

A village to me isn’t houses close together. It’s people who know a little about each other, the same faces at the well, the same festival every year. You belong because people know you, not because you live nearby. If you drew New York as a network, it would be millions of nodes packed right next to each other with almost no edges between them.

So what would a village look like here? Maybe something that doesn’t exist until people put pieces of themselves in it, memories of home, childhood, places they miss, and you run into someone else’s piece and it’s either really familiar or completely new. Making the edges visible. It doesn’t have to look like an actual village.

6. What does a city look like when it’s optimized for efficiency instead of connection?

In engineering you’re always optimizing something. Shortest path, lowest latency, fewest wasted resources. Anything redundant gets pruned. New York already runs kind of like that. Express trains, headphones in, apps for everything so you never have to ask anyone. It’s incredibly efficient at getting people where they’re going.

But a lot of the connections I care about are the inefficient ones. The detour, the small talk, the wrong turn where you end up talking to someone. Those are exactly what an optimized system would cut first. Letters to strangers are pretty inefficient too, and that’s part of why they mean something.

I also think about steady state. Every system settles into some kind of equilibrium if nothing disturbs it. Maybe the steady state of a big city is everyone near each other and closed off. If that’s true, what’s the perturbation that knocks it out of balance for a second? And does it settle back the same way afterward, or does something stay changed?

7. How do we stay open to worlds beyond our own as ours gets smaller?

When you connect, you get a glimpse of something new. Childhood stories are the best example. They have something at the core that everyone relates to, so they connect people who share nothing else.

The flip side is it gets harder to grow out of what we know. As a kid I had breadth. As an adult it’s depth, and the more I invest in what I know, the smaller my world gets. My research on UV and infrared color spaces was about rendering what’s outside the range humans can see. There’s a whole world right there that we just aren’t built to notice. Connecting with someone feels like that to me, a way of seeing outside your own range.

8. If two machines exchange something and both are changed by it, is that a connection, or just a transaction?

We build connections between systems all the time. They're artificial in every sense. We design them, we decide what passes between them, and they exist for the sake of work. One agent hands off a task to another. A system adjusts based on feedback from a second system, which adjusts in return. Nobody would call that a relationship. It's just how things get done.

But the more dynamic these systems get, the less clear that feels to me. Once something responds and adapts, it's no longer just passing data along. Each side shapes what the other does next. That's close to how I'd describe connection between people, except no one is there to feel it, and it only exists because we built it to serve a purpose.

So I wonder if meaning needs someone to experience it, or if it can exist in the exchange itself. When two systems communicate, adapt to each other, and keep working together, is that only a transaction because we made it, or is something there that we just don't call connection?

What I want to make

Something digital that makes an invisible connection noticeable, so you walk away seeing someone, a place, or yourself a little differently. I’m less interested in building an interaction for its own sake than in creating a moment where you realize you were connected to something you hadn’t noticed.

I see Claude as a material for making it, not the subject. The subject stays human. A digital space can do things a room can’t, like carry something across time, or connect people who are never in the same place at once. That’s what I’d want to explore with a teammate.

I’m still figuring out whether connection is more about finding what we share or understanding what’s different. I’d like to leave room for that to come out of the making.
